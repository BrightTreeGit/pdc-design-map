# Blue-sky strategy and development pipeline (24 Oct 2026)

A working paper: where the design map could go as an integrated, long-term land-management and permaculture platform, how to rank the ideas, what to research, which services to plug into, how the group network could grow, and where funding and partners might come from.

Companion pages: `app/00-idea-register.md` (the single list of scored ideas; new rows A42–A72 added today), `tools/roadmap.md` (knowledge base, map and network items), `bright-tree/07-vision-and-strategy.md` (why and the three horizons), `tools/future-features.md` (parked detail).

## 2. A better way to rank ideas

The register’s score, P = (2×Impact + Leverage) − (Effort + Compute), has worked well for picking quick wins. For a multi-year pipeline it needs three more lenses, so each idea now also carries:

| Lens                      | Scale                                            | Question                                                                                                                                                             |
|---------------------------|--------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **X** Complexity and risk | 1–5                                              | How many unknowns, outside dependencies and ways to get it wrong? (Effort is time; complexity is uncertainty.)                                                       |
| **R** Research needed     | none / desk / field / trial                      | What has to be learned before building? Field = measure on a property; trial = run for a season and compare.                                                         |
| **F** Funding fit         | 0–3                                              | 0 = nobody funds this · 1 = fits a grant theme loosely · 2 = fits a current program · 3 = fits a program and makes other grants easier to win (monitoring, evidence) |
| **S** Stage               | Idea → Research → Spike → Trial → Live → Package | Where it is in the pipeline. Nothing goes Live without Brett’s “approve for live”. Package = ready to offer to other properties or a funder.                         |

**Reading the matrix.** Sort by P as before, then: - high P, low X: build next (quick wins) - high P, high X: spike first, one session, with a kill test - high F: line up with the funding calendar (section 7), even if P is middling, because a funded build pays for the unfunded ones - R = field or trial: start the measurement now, because a season takes a season

**Value for the network, not just one site.** Leverage 3 now means “any block in Australia **or** any site in the group network benefits”. The property directory, swap ledger, species log and shared layers all score 3.

## 2b. Innovation management, the practical bits

Borrowed only where they change what we do on Monday morning. Each is one line of theory and one of how we use it.

| Approach                                                                    | The idea                                                                                                                                   | How we use it here                                                                                                                                                                                                                                      |
|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Three Horizons** (McKinsey; Sharpe’s version for transitions)             | Run today’s core, the next business and the long-range options at the same time, with separate measures                                    | Already the vision doc’s H1 prove the place, H2 prove the method, H3 grow the network. Each register row carries its horizon so H3 work never crowds out H1 maintenance.                                                                                |
| **Stage-Gate** (Cooper)                                                     | Ideas pass through stages with a go, kill or hold decision at each gate                                                                    | Our pipeline stages (Idea → Research → Spike → Trial → Live → Package) are the stages; the four gates (privacy, data rights, IP, reversible) plus “approve for live” are the gates. A spike needs a written kill test before it starts.                 |
| **Lean Startup** (Ries): build, measure, learn                              | Ship the smallest thing that tests the riskiest assumption, measure real use, then decide                                                  | The trial map is the minimum viable product; group comments and the notebook are the measurement. Every big bet names its riskiest assumption first (A29: can the page stay fast? A57: will anyone read the sensors?).                                  |
| **Jobs to be Done** (Christensen, Ulwick)                                   | People hire a product to do a job; design for the job, not the feature                                                                     | Each feature row should state the job in the landholder’s words: “know how many tanks I need” (Bronwen), “get through the sewer without killing trees”, “book a crew for a week”. The client interview and brief tracker capture the jobs.              |
| **Diffusion of innovations** (Rogers)                                       | Adoption depends on relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, trialability and observability; early adopters carry it to the majority | The group leaders are the early adopters; the trial map is trialability; the A3 sheet and graphics packs are observability. Keep the Essentials tool level simple so complexity does not block the late majority.                                       |
| **Kano model**                                                              | Features are basic (expected), performance (more is better) or delighters (unexpected joy); delighters become basics over time             | Terrain, contours and water are now basics; tank and cost calculators are performance; the natural-pattern tools and photo history are delighters. Budget at least one delighter per quarter and expect last year’s to become a basic.                  |
| **Technology Readiness Levels** (NASA, used by Australian research funders) | TRL 1 basic idea to TRL 9 proven in operation                                                                                              | Map our stages onto TRLs for grant forms: Research = TRL 2–3, Spike = 4, Trial = 5–6, Live on one property = 7, Live across the network = 8, Package = 9. Funders ask for this number.                                                                  |
| **Real options**                                                            | A small early investment buys the right, not the obligation, to invest more later                                                          | A one-session spike is an option on a big bet. Several cheap options beat one large commitment, which is why the register prefers spikes to full builds for anything with X ≥ 3.                                                                        |
| **Weighted Shortest Job First** (SAFe)                                      | Priority = cost of delay ÷ job size; deadlines and decaying value raise cost of delay                                                      | Use it when the P score ties or when a funding deadline exists: a middling feature due for a grant in February beats a better one with no deadline. F and the funding calendar are our cost-of-delay signal.                                            |
| **Double Diamond** (UK Design Council)                                      | Discover and define the problem before developing and delivering the answer                                                                | The course’s own method already does this: client interview and site analysis (discover, define), then design and staging (develop, deliver). The peer review is the check between the two diamonds.                                                    |
| **Theory of change / logic model**                                          | Inputs → activities → outputs → outcomes → impact, with the assumptions written down                                                       | Every grant application wants one. The map can print it: activities (works drawn), outputs (metres, hectares, plants), outcomes (habitat score, erosion class, tank reliability), impact (corridor, water quality). Build the template once (A53, A61). |
| **Living lab** (European Network of Living Labs)                            | A real place where users, researchers and developers co-create and test in daily life                                                      | Bright Tree as the living lab: residents and volunteers test tools on real jobs; universities bring students to the research questions in section 6; the method stays with Bright Tree. This is also the phrase funders and universities recognise.     |
| **Communities of practice** (Wenger)                                        | People who share a craft learn together through regular exchange and shared artefacts                                                      | The group network is one: the peer-review checklist, swap ledger, species log and course days are the shared artefacts and rhythm. Keep a light steward role so it survives after the course.                                                           |
| **Open innovation** (Chesbrough)                                            | Use outside ideas and paths to market as well as inside ones; share what is not your edge                                                  | Open the lessons, tools and anonymised outcomes (data commons); keep the analysis method. Pull in public data, citizen science and student research rather than building everything.                                                                    |
| **Platform and network effects**                                            | A platform grows in value with each member; design the exchange, not just the tool                                                         | Each new site adds data the others use (species, prices, outcomes). Design for the minimum viable network: eight sites is enough to start if the exchange (swap ledger, species log) is live.                                                           |
| **Portfolio balance** (70/20/10)                                            | Most effort on the core, some on adjacent, a little on transformational                                                                    | A rough rule for each quarter: 70% on live-map features and site work, 20% on network and packaging, 10% on blue-sky spikes (game, sensors, tiles).                                                                                                     |

**What to skip:** heavy stage documentation, formal innovation committees, and any method that needs more admin than the team has. Two people and a group of volunteers need a register, a monthly review and clear kill tests, not a process manual.

## 3. Blue-sky features, by domain

Each item has a register number (A42 onward are new today), rough scores (I·L·E·X·K·F) and a one-line why. Full rows are in the register.

### 3.1 Resource mapping and audit

The site analysis lecture (s15) asked for a resource audit first: what is on site, then what is nearby. The map can do this properly. - **A42 On-site resource inventory layer** (5·3·2·2·0·2): rock and boulders, standing timber by species and size (from LiDAR canopy plus a field tag), clay for dams (from wetness and soil tests), sand, existing mulch sources, water sources, salvage materials. Each with a quantity estimate and a “could be used for” list. Why: Mollison’s “every resource is an asset waiting for a function”. - **A43 Biomass and mulch yield estimate** (4·3·3·3·1·2): from canopy growth rates and the plant library, how much chop-and-drop, firewood and mulch a property grows a year, by zone. Feeds the syntropic schedule and the firewood plan. - **A44 Resource-flow diagram per property** (4·3·3·2·0·1): inputs (water, feed, mulch, fuel, money) and outputs (food, timber, waste, water leaving) as a flow picture, with the wastes that could become inputs highlighted. This is the “industrial ecology” check from the vision doc, drawn. - **A45 Nearby resources ring** (3·3·2·2·1·1): the next zone out: free arborist mulch, sawmills, quarries, tip shops, nurseries, landcare groups, markets, within a chosen drive time. Links into the network’s shared resources.

### 3.2 Planning and scheduling

- **A46 Seasonal calendar per site** (5·3·3·2·0·2): plant, prune, chop-and-drop, harvest, mulch, fire-prep and burn-off windows for every species and element on the map, from the plant library and the climate record. Shown as a 12-month wheel on the A3 sheet and as tasks in the Plan tab. Why: the single most-used thing on a working property.
- **A47 Stage plan as a timeline** (4·3·2·1·0·2): the existing stages (Now, Years 1–2, 3–5, 5–10) laid out as a Gantt-style timeline with dependencies (water before trees, access before buildings, from the scale of permanence). Flags anything scheduled out of order.
- **A48 Weather-triggered tasks** (4·3·3·3·2·1): Bureau of Meteorology forecast feeds a “this week” list: rain coming, so plant the tubestock; dry week ahead, so burn the pile or spray; heat, so check tank levels. Needs a small scheduled job, not the page itself.
- **A49 Work-team scheduling** (4·3·4·3·1·2): group the season’s jobs into a week for a volunteer or paid team, with hours, tools and materials per job, from A46 and A37 (syntropic forecasting). The Bright Tree volunteer and resident model needs this.
- **A50 Maintenance-load score** (4·3·2·2·0·1): hours per year a design needs to look after, from each element’s care rating in the plant library and its size. Bronwen’s brief (“easy care at 87”) made this concrete. Shows on the A3 sheet beside cost.

### 3.3 Cost estimating and budgets

- **A51 Bill of quantities from the drawing** (5·3·3·2·0·2): fence lengths and post counts, swale and dam earth volumes (already computed), mulch area and depth to cubic metres, plant counts by spacing, pipe lengths, tank sizes, path and track metres. Absorbs the parked A19 estimator.
- **A52 Price book** (4·3·2·2·1·1): a shared, dated list of unit prices (tubestock, mulch per m³, fencing per metre, tank per litre, excavator per hour, poly pipe per metre) that every site uses, with each site able to override. The Gemini price research (G3) seeds it. Prices expire, so each has a “checked” date.
- **A53 Stage budgets and cash flow** (4·3·2·1·0·3): cost per stage from A51 × A52, plus the maintenance hours from A50, as a table and a simple cash-flow line over ten years. The co-contribution column is what grant applications ask for, which is why F is 3.
- **A54 Grant and co-funding tracker** (3·3·2·1·0·3): which features of the design map onto which grant themes (habitat, water quality, erosion, fire, climate adaptation), what the landholder’s 50% in-kind would be, deadlines. Lives in Notion, reads from the map.

### 3.4 Monitoring over years

This is where the platform becomes a land-management record rather than a design tool, and where most funders want evidence. - **A55 Photo points and repeat photos** (4·3·2·1·0·3): fixed points with bearing, a reference photo and a reminder each season; before-and-after sliders like the aerial history, but on the ground. Cheapest monitoring there is and required by most grant acquittals. - **A56 Satellite greenness per season** (4·3·3·3·2·3): Sentinel-2 vegetation index from Digital Earth Australia, 10 m, every few weeks, free and open: shows drought stress, regrowth after works, and which paddock greens up first. Part of the Stage 2 package (A11). - **A57 Sensor feeds** (3·3·4·4·3·2): rain gauge, soil moisture and tank level loggers posting to the map (a small off-grid gateway at Bright Tree first). Turns the tank test from a model into a measurement. Research: cheap LoRa sensors and a Home Assistant or MQTT bridge; keep the data on the property per the data-sovereignty value. - **A58 Species performance log** (4.5·3·3·2·0·3): the roadmap’s N3 made into a map feature: each planted thing gets a dated outcome (thriving, struggling, dead, cause), feeding the plant library’s climate-fit column with evidence from real sites. The network’s data moat. - **A59 Soil log** (3·3·2·1·0·2): pH, texture, slake and infiltration tests at fixed points over time, with the test method on the card so volunteers do it the same way. - **A60 Repeat drone surveys** (4·2·3·3·2·2): a yearly orthophoto and canopy model from the Mavic, differenced against LiDAR: tree growth, erosion scars, weed spread, firebreak condition. Bright Tree already has the drone and the processing (WebODM).

### 3.5 Ecology, fire and compliance

- **A61 Habitat and corridor score** (4.5·3·3·3·1·3): habitat trees (from LiDAR height and age), connectivity to neighbouring bush, koala food trees, water, understorey layers, weeds, as a score with the gaps listed. Lines up with Land for Wildlife, council environment grants and the Nature Repair Market’s assessment methods.
- **A62 Fuel and fire plan sheet** (4·3·3·3·1·2): fuel structure from LiDAR, asset protection zones, water points, access and refuge on one printable plan, in the format rural fire services expect.
- **A18 Approvals checklist** (parked, unchanged): council and state overlays flagged per feature. Still waits on overlay data per block.

### 3.6 People, learning and enterprise

- **A35 Work orders by phone**, **A22 Field mode**, **A15 Learning game**: unchanged; the field mode becomes more valuable once photo points and species logs exist, because it is how they get filled in.
- **A63 Skills and hours register** (3·3·2·1·0·2): who can do what across the network (chainsaw ticket, excavator, grafting, bookkeeping), hours contributed, for the volunteer model and for grant in-kind reporting.
- **A64 Enterprise and yield forecast** (3.5·3·4·3·0·1): expected harvest by month and year from the plant library and planting dates, product calendar for a retreat or farm gate, rough income. The roadmap’s Enterprise tab.

## 5. Cross-site network

The group is already a network of eight properties across two states and 50–490 m of elevation. The platform should make that useful rather than just visible.

1.  **Shared layers across neighbours and the group** (A29 tiles, A30 neighbour sites): catchments, fire runs, wildlife corridors and weed fronts do not stop at boundaries. Each landholder controls what shows beyond their own fence.
2.  **Resource sharing** (A32, “Bridges of Sustainability”): offers and wants for mulch, cuttings, seed, trellis, tools, labour and machine days, private by default, with pick-up at course days and working bees. Starts as the swap ledger (done) and a tool library (N5).
3.  **Species performance across sites** (A58, N3): the same plant tried at 50 m and 450 m, on sand and on clay, logged the same way. Within two years the network knows things no nursery label does.
4.  **Work crews and skills** (N4, A49, A63): a permablitz calendar; crews rotate between sites; skills register so the right people turn up.
5.  **Bulk buying and shared nursery** (N6): tubestock, rock dust and mulch at group prices; a propagation hub (Bright Tree’s nursery, Bellbunya’s education centre) grows the network’s plants from its own seed bank (A33).
6.  **Design peer review** (N8, done): the checklist, run before each presentation and then yearly.
7.  **Data commons** (A21, later): anonymised designs and outcomes, no addresses, opt-in, so the next PDC starts from evidence.
8.  **Governance**: the vision doc’s community model (short written why, stewards, quarterly reviews, consent-based data sharing) applies to the network too. Decide early who owns the shared data and how a member leaves and takes theirs with them.

## 5b. Industrial ecology and symbiosis: scaling past agriculture

Industrial ecology treats a region’s farms, businesses, towns and ecosystems as one metabolism: materials, water, energy and nutrients flow between them, and waste is a flow that has not found its user yet. **Industrial symbiosis** is the practice: one operation’s by-product becomes another’s input, usually within a short drive. Kalundborg in Denmark (power station steam heating a refinery, fish farm and town; gypsum to a plasterboard plant) is the textbook case; Australia has run the same idea through NISP-style facilitated programs in Kwinana, Gladstone and regional Victoria. The permaculture version is Mollison’s “the problem is the solution” at district scale.

**Why it fits this platform.** The map already knows each property’s flows: roof water, biomass growth, mulch, manure, timber, food, fire fuel, greywater. The same model, pointed at the sawmill, the café, the school, the council depot and the brewery down the road, finds the exchanges that a farm alone cannot. Everything in section 3.1 (resource inventory, biomass yield, resource-flow diagram, nearby resources) is the on-farm half of an industrial-symbiosis tool.

| Flow                                                | Who has it as a waste                                            | Who wants it as an input                                            | The map’s part                                                                    |
|-----------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Wood chip, arborist mulch, sawdust                  | tree services, sawmills, council parks                           | every food forest and track garden; mushroom logs and beds; biochar | mulch demand per site from A51; a “mulch wanted” pin on the nearby-resources ring |
| Food and brewery waste, coffee grounds, spent grain | cafés, breweries, markets, the retreat’s kitchen                 | compost, worms, chickens, mushroom substrate                        | nutrient-cycling capacity per site (compost bays, chickens drawn)                 |
| Manure and bedding                                  | horse properties, dairies, the group’s own animals               | compost and syntropic rows                                          | distance and quantity matching                                                    |
| Greywater and treated wastewater                    | houses, the retreat, the township without sewer (North Arm Cove) | fruit forests, firebreak gardens, reed beds                         | treatment and reuse elements already on the map (Bright Tree’s system)            |
| Heat and power                                      | solar surplus by day, micro-hydro, biomass boilers               | dehydrators, nurseries, hot water, a shared cool room               | A14 hydro, solar on the map; a demand side to add                                 |
| Carbon and biodiversity                             | regrowth forest, corridors                                       | buyers under the Land Restoration Fund and Nature Repair Market     | canopy growth and habitat score (A61) as the measurement                          |
| Skills, machines and labour                         | each property’s idle hours and tools                             | the network’s work calendar                                         | skills register (A63), tool library (N5)                                          |
| Salvage and building materials                      | demolition, tip shops, the containers and tin already on sites   | zomes, trellis, retaining walls, chicken runs                       | resource inventory (A42) with a “salvage” class                                   |

**A two-step route to scale** 1. **Symbiosis within the network (2027).** Eight properties, their flows and their wants on one private layer; a monthly match list (“Bellbunya’s kitchen scraps to Bright Tree’s chickens; Bright Tree’s chip to Annwn’s beds”). This is the swap ledger grown up (A32), and it is the pilot a funder would pay for. 2. **A district metabolism map (2028, with a partner).** Add the non-farm actors in one catchment or shire: businesses, schools, the council depot and the waste facility. Flows are entered by the actors themselves, kept private by default, with matches suggested rather than published. Councils and regional bodies (Sunshine Coast Council’s circular-economy work, Burnett Mary Regional Group) run programs in exactly this space, and the Queensland Government’s waste and circular-economy strategies fund facilitation. A university industrial-ecology group (UNSW, UTS and UQ all have them) would partner on the method.

**Measures that matter** (so it is more than a marketplace): tonnes diverted from landfill, kilolitres of potable water replaced, nutrients kept in the district (nitrogen and phosphorus in compost rather than trucked fertiliser), kilometres not driven, and dollars saved on each side. The map can total these from the ledger, which is the evidence for the next grant.

**Guard-rails:** biosecurity (weed seed and disease move with mulch and manure; the plant master list’s weed column and a “clean source” flag), food-safety rules on waste to animals, council rules on greywater and compost at scale, and the same privacy onion: a business’s waste stream is commercial information until it chooses to share it.

New register rows: **A65 Network symbiosis match list** (4·3·3·X2·K0·F3: the swap ledger grown into flows and monthly matches) and **A66 District metabolism map** (4.5·3·5·X4·K2·F3: non-farm actors, private flows, suggested matches; partner-led, grant-funded).

## 6c. Climate change: what the map can model

The map already runs on 30 years of daily weather. The next step is to run the same tools on the next 30 years, so every design can be tested against the climate it will actually live in.

**The data.** Queensland’s Future Climate dataset (Long Paddock, CC BY 4.0) gives downscaled projections on a 10 km grid for 2030, 2050, 2070 and 2090 under low, moderate and high emissions scenarios (CMIP6 SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0; CMIP5 RCP4.5 and RCP8.5), with daily bias-corrected simulations, heatwave and extreme-rain indices, drought indices and McArthur fire-danger indices. The national Climate Change in Australia portal covers the NSW sites. Both are free.

| What to model                                           | How                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                | Which tools change                                                         |
|---------------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **A67 Future-climate tank and dam test**                | Re-run the daily tank, dam and water-balance models on the 2050 bias-corrected daily series as well as the 1995–2025 record; report both numbers side by side (“90% today, 82% in 2050 under SSP2-4.5”)                                                                            | tank calculator, dam and pond calculator, water balance, every site report |
| **A68 Heat and chill stress on the plant list**         | Projected days over 35 °C, mean minimums and chill hours per site in 2050; flag plants in the library whose chill or heat limits fall outside the new range (low-chill plums at Willow Vale, avocado and macadamia heat stress, stone fruit chill)                                 | plant master list, guild planner, planting-niche map                       |
| **A69 Fire weather and fuel trend**                     | Projected FFDI and dry-season length layered on the LiDAR fuel structure and terrain fire run; shows how many more very-high fire days a site faces and where fuel reduction and asset protection matter most                                                                      | fire plan sheet (A62), bushfire site check                                 |
| **A70 Design-storm and erosion under a wetter extreme** | Scale the design-storm (A40) by the projected change in extreme daily rain; re-run erosion risk with the heavier storms; size swale spillways and overflow paths for 2050, not 1990                                                                                                | swales, dams, erosion overlay, rain-garden sizing                          |
| **Drought run length and food security**                | Projected longest dry spells and SPEI drought index per site against the seasonal calendar: which months lose production, which storage carries through, and how much of the household’s food the site still yields in a bad year (Bellbunya’s 65–80% target under a 2050 drought) | seasonal calendar (A46), food self-reliance tracker (A31)                  |
| **Carbon in the regrowth**                              | Canopy growth from the LiDAR pairs converted to biomass and carbon per hectare, projected forward; what the forest stores and what a managed food forest adds                                                                                                                      | canopy layers, Land Restoration Fund and Nature Repair Market evidence     |
| **Resilience score per design**                         | A one-page summary: water security in a 2050 drought, heat tolerance of the plantings, fire exposure, flood and erosion exposure, and how many of the five food groups the site still produces. The permaculture answer to “is this design future-proof?”                          | A3 sheet, grant applications                                               |

Two honest limits. Projections at 10 km smooth out the hills that make these sites what they are (the cold-air pooling, the rain shadow behind the Blackall Range), so they should shift the historical record rather than replace it: apply the projected change in each month’s rain and temperature to the real 30-year daily series, then run the tools. And a single scenario is a guess; always show two, so the design is tested against a range rather than a forecast.

**Why this is also the funding story.** Every climate, drought and resilience program asks the same question in its guidelines: how does this project make the land, the food supply or the community more resilient to a changing climate, and how will you measure it? A site report that shows the tank test, the plant stress check and the fire exposure for today and for 2050 is that answer, with numbers.

## 6d. Syntropic systems: a research agenda

The course’s syntropic sessions (s16, s20) and the owner’s forecasting idea (A37) point at a real gap: syntropic agroforestry is spreading in Australia faster than the evidence about it. The Australian trials that exist are young and few: James Cook University has run a Mars-funded cocoa plot north-west of Cairns since about 2021, reporting improved soil structure and no pesticide use but no saving in labour, and that is close to the whole of the published Australian record. The group’s eight sites, with LiDAR, daily climate and a shared species log, could add more than most single research plots.

## 6e. Carbon sequestration modelling

Carbon is the one measure that funders, markets and the group’s own values all share, and the map already holds the two things a carbon model needs: how much woody biomass is there (LiDAR canopy height and cover, 2008 and 2022) and how fast it is changing. What is missing is the conversion to tonnes and a way to project it.

**Four pools, four methods**

| Pool                                                                         | How to estimate it                                                                                                                                                                                                                      | Data we have                                                                                                             | Data to add                                                                                                               |
|------------------------------------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Standing forest biomass**                                                  | Canopy height and cover to above-ground biomass using published allometric relations for Australian eucalypt and rainforest types, then × 0.5 for carbon, × 3.67 for CO₂-e; add roots at about 25%                                      | LiDAR canopy height model per 2 m cell, vegetation structure classes, regional ecosystem type (from the extras download) | Species mix by patch (field tags), a few plot measurements to calibrate (diameter at breast height on 20 trees per class) |
| **Forest growth**                                                            | Height change 2008 → 2022 converted the same way gives tonnes per hectare per year, by patch and class                                                                                                                                  | Canopy growth layer already computed for Bright Tree                                                                     | The same LiDAR pairs for the other sites where two surveys exist                                                          |
| **New plantings** (food forests, syntropic rows, revegetation, shelterbelts) | FullCAM, the Commonwealth’s carbon accounting model used by every Australian carbon method, run for the planting type, species mix and location; or a simpler look-up curve per species group from the plant library for the first pass | Planting dates, species and areas drawn on the map; local climate record                                                 | FullCAM runs for the site (free software, a day to learn); growth curves calibrated by the drone repeat surveys           |
| **Soil carbon**                                                              | Baseline soil tests (carbon %, bulk density, depth) at fixed points, repeated every 3–5 years; project with conservative rates for mulch, compost and cover change                                                                      | Soil log points planned (A59)                                                                                            | Lab tests at about \$60–100 a sample; a rigorous baseline needs 10+ samples per stratum                                   |

**What the map would show** (A71 Carbon layer and ledger) - A carbon map: tonnes of CO₂-e per hectare by patch today, from the LiDAR, with the big trees and the tall-forest class standing out. - A growth ledger: tonnes added per year across the property from 2008 to 2022, then projected with the growth rates and with each planned planting’s curve, under today’s climate and the 2050 scenarios (A67’s data). - A per-design number: “this stage of the design adds about N t CO₂-e over 25 years”, beside its cost and maintenance hours on the A3 sheet. - Avoided emissions and substitution, kept separate and labelled honestly: fuel not burnt because mulch is grown on site, fertiliser not trucked because nutrients cycle, timber grown rather than bought. These are real but they are not sequestration and markets do not pay for them.

**Markets and methods it has to line up with** - **ACCU Scheme** (Clean Energy Regulator): the environmental plantings and reforestation methods use FullCAM; human-induced regeneration has been closed to new projects; a new integrated farm and land management method is in development. Minimum viable project size is in the tens of hectares, so a single property here is below it, but a network aggregation might not be. - **Queensland Land Restoration Fund**: pays for ACCU projects with co-benefits (habitat, water quality, social); the habitat score (A61) and the water layers are the co-benefit evidence. - **Nature Repair Market**: biodiversity rather than carbon, but the same monitoring; a project can stack both. - **Voluntary and in-setting claims**: a retreat or farm gate claiming “carbon-positive” needs the same numbers and the same honesty about what is sequestration and what is avoidance.

**Research questions to add to section 6** - How well do the published eucalypt allometrics fit the regrowth blackbutt and ironbark forest here? Twenty measured trees per class would tell us, and the LiDAR gives the heights for free. - What does a subtropical syntropic row sequester in its first ten years, above and below ground, compared with a conventional orchard and with regrowth left alone? Nobody has a local number; the group’s plantings, measured from year one, could be the first. - How much carbon does deep mulching and chop-and-drop add to these low-fertility sandstone soils, and how fast? The soil log answers it, slowly.

**Guard-rails:** report ranges, not single figures (allometry alone carries ±30%); keep the method and its assumptions on the layer legend; never let a carbon number drive a design decision that the water, fire or habitat layers argue against; and remember that the biggest carbon action on these blocks is keeping the forest standing and out of a hot fire, which is the fire plan’s job, not a planting’s.

New register rows: **A71 Carbon layer and ledger** (4.5·3·3·X3·K1·F3: LiDAR biomass to CO₂-e, growth ledger, per-design number, FullCAM for new plantings) and **A72 Carbon field calibration** (3·2·2·X2·K0·F2: 20 measured trees per forest class plus soil baselines at the soil-log points).

Building A51, A53, A55 and A61 is therefore not just features: it is the grant application kit.
